Slide 4: Different stakeholders need different reasons to engage. · 100 seconds A single project announcement can serve several audiences, but it should not ask all of them to take the same action. Buyers need confidence that the company has relevant experience for the opportunity they are considering. Consultants and engineers need enough technical clarity to understand applicability. Institutional stakeholders may care about sector development and documented contributions. Partners need clarity about complementary capabilities and responsibilities. I would create a priority-account list jointly with business development and map buying roles within it. That list should include the account owner, sector, market, current relationship, relevant evidence and intended next action. The marketing team can then decide whether a technical briefing, an executive perspective, an event conversation or a project case study best supports the relationship. This is where brand activity becomes commercially useful without reducing every communication to a lead form. A relevant consultant reading and reusing an approved technical case study may be a meaningful outcome even before an inquiry occurs. The account owner should validate that interpretation. Marketing should record engagement consistently and avoid claiming that a single content interaction caused a later contract award. Slide 5: Five pillars. One marketing operating system. · 100 seconds The five pillars connect strategy with day-to-day execution. Audience and brand intelligence sets priorities. Technical storytelling turns approved experience into useful evidence. Digital discovery makes that evidence easier to find and understand. Commercial and institutional engagement helps teams use it in relevant relationships. AI operations and measurement supports all four rather than operating as a separate content factory. For example, an account insight can identify a recurring question about storage integration. Engineering can approve the factual answer and project scope. Marketing can turn that into a project page, a technical briefing and a LinkedIn narrative. Business development can use the same approved material in an account conversation. Measurement then tells us which audience engaged and whether the interaction progressed. As Section Manager, I would own the common brief, editorial standards and review cadence. Sector specialists own technical accuracy. Business development owns account qualification and follow-up. A weekly production review resolves blocked assets, a monthly review connects activity to account engagement, and a quarterly review adjusts investment. AI helps summarise evidence, prepare variants and reduce repetitive production work. It cannot authorise technical claims or determine that an account is commercially qualified. The value is a more dependable process across the portfolio.

Slide 10: Measurement follows the buying journey. · 100 seconds The scorecard follows the buying journey. Relevant awareness asks whether we are reaching people who matter to the chosen accounts and sectors. Evidence engagement asks whether those people use the material. Commercial progression asks whether business development accepts the inquiry or meeting as relevant. Operating quality checks that the team can produce accurate material reliably. Before reporting a percentage, we need to agree its denominator, event definition, source and owner. For example, an inquiry acceptance rate is accepted inquiries divided by all recorded inquiries in the same reporting period, with duplicate and spam handling defined. An engaged visit requires a specific agreed event, not an arbitrary label applied after the campaign. I would set numerical targets after the baseline review at day thirty, using account size, historical performance, available budget and sales capacity. Invented growth promises would weaken the plan. We should also separate opportunities that marketing sourced from those where marketing influenced an existing relationship. That distinction prevents double counting and does not claim causation. Every monthly review should end with a decision about targeting, content, the website journey or follow-up. For long procurement cycles, six months may show qualified engagement and opportunity progression before it shows attributable revenue.

Slide 11: Ninety days to establish proof. Six months to improve the system. · 100 seconds The first ninety days should produce a working system and an investment decision. Month one establishes account priorities, the evidence library, channel ownership and measurement access. That month also resolves technical and client approvals so the team can produce material from a stable brief. The five-week campaign pilot sits within months two and three. Its first week confirms permissions and approvals, its second produces the assets, and its third launches the Bisha story. The fourth introduces Jazan and rooftop solar, with the fifth reserved for optimisation and review. These are relative campaign weeks rather than a promise tied to calendar dates. If a project’s permissions are incomplete, its publication waits while approved work continues. By day ninety, I want an approved source library, a functioning campaign hub, reliable inquiry routing and a review of relevant account response. The review should determine whether to continue the same audience, revise the story or pause spend. Months four through six improve the best-supported activity and extend the content system to other business lines where commercial priorities justify it. The five-week activation and six-month programme therefore have distinct purposes: the activation tests the content and distribution, while the longer period tests the operating rhythm and commercial contribution.
